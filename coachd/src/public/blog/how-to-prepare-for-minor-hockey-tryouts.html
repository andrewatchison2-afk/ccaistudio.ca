HOW TO PREPARE FOR MINOR HOCKEY TRYOUTS
(WHAT ACTUALLY GETS NOTICED)

Tryout season is stressful for every hockey family. Your player has put in the ice time, you've driven to early morning practices all winter, and now it comes down to two or three sessions where a coaching staff decides who makes the team.

Here's what most parents don't know: coaches aren't just watching skill. They're watching a very specific set of things — and most players never prepare for the right ones.

This guide covers what minor hockey coaches actually evaluate at tryouts, and what your player can do in the weeks leading up to them.

What Coaches Are Actually Watching

Before getting into preparation, it helps to know what evaluators are looking for. At most Rep levels (A through AAA), the primary filters are:

Skating first, always. Edge control, first-step quickness, and the ability to stop and accelerate cleanly are evaluated within the first few shifts. A player with average hands but explosive edges will almost always beat a player with great hands and flat feet. Coaches can develop skill — they can't easily develop skating.

Compete level without the puck. Evaluators watch what players do when they don't have the puck. Are they positioning correctly? Are they backchecking hard? Are they ready to receive? This is especially true for centers, who are being evaluated on two-way play from the moment they step on the ice.

Hockey sense under pressure. Tryout drills are designed to create decisions. Coaches want to see whether a player reads the play correctly when fatigued and under pressure — not just in a controlled drill at practice.

Coachability. How a player responds to instruction during a tryout — whether they adjust, whether they listen, whether they communicate — is visible to experienced evaluators.

The 4-Week Tryout Preparation Framework

Week 4 (Four Weeks Out): Build the Physical Foundation

Off-ice training in this window should focus on lower body power and lateral explosiveness — the physical qualities that show up most visibly on skates.

Key exercises:

  • Lateral banded walks — 3 sets of 15 steps each direction
  • Single-leg box jumps — 3 sets of 6 reps per leg
  • Skater jumps — 3 sets of 10 reps

No gym required. A resistance band and a sturdy box or step is all you need.

Recovery matters too. After every session, 5 minutes of hip flexor and hamstring work keeps young athletes loose through a heavy training block.

Week 3: Sharpen the On-Ice Skills That Get Noticed

If your player has ice access, focus specifically on the skills evaluators see first.

Edge control drills:

  • Inside-outside edge slaloms (3 sets × 4 lengths)
  • Stop-and-explode starts from both directions (5 reps × 2 directions)
  • C-cuts at top speed with immediate direction change

The goal isn't new technique — it's making existing technique automatic under fatigue. Tryouts are tiring. The players who execute cleanly when tired are the ones who make teams.

For centers specifically: Work on dot-to-dot pivots and the transition from defensive zone coverage to offensive zone attack. At Rep AA and above, this transition is one of the primary things coaches evaluate for centers.

Week 2: Sharpen the Mental Game

Physical preparation should taper slightly. The focus shifts to mental readiness and game sense.

Watch game film together — even local Midget or Junior footage works. Watch specifically for what players do without the puck: positioning, backcheck timing, how players create space before receiving a pass.

Talk about compete level explicitly. Many young players don't understand that backchecking hard on a meaningless drill is being evaluated. Have the conversation: every shift, every drill, every moment on the ice is being watched.

Week 1 (Tryout Week): Fuel, Sleep, and Calm

The week of tryouts is not the time for new training. This week is about showing up in the best possible condition.

Nutrition:

  • Pre-skate: Fast-digesting carbs + small protein, 60–90 minutes before. Banana and peanut butter on toast works well.
  • Post-skate: Chocolate milk or Greek yogurt within 30 minutes. The carb-protein combination is what muscles need to recover between sessions.
  • Avoid: Energy drinks, heavy or greasy food within 2 hours of skating, anything unfamiliar the night before.

Sleep: Aim for 9–10 hours the night before each tryout session. Reaction time, decision-making, and mood are all directly tied to sleep quality.

The morning of: Keep it calm and routine. Avoid over-coaching in the car. Let them skate.

Position-Specific Tips

Centers are evaluated on two-way play, faceoff positioning, and how they support both wingers. Coaches want to see a center who reads the play and is always in the right position.

Wingers need to show speed on the forecheck and discipline on the backcheck. Speed without compete doesn't make Rep rosters.

Defensemen are watched for gap control, calm under pressure, and the first pass out of the zone. Panicking with the puck is the fastest way to get cut.

Goalies are evaluated on positioning, rebound control, and communication — not flashy saves. A goalie who directs traffic and controls their crease stands out.

The One Thing That Separates Players Who Make Teams

Compete level every single shift.

Coaches see dozens of players with similar skill sets. The player who backchecks hard on shift three when they're tired, who wins a puck battle along the boards in a drill that "doesn't matter," who stays engaged and ready between shifts — that player stands out.

Skill can be developed over a season. Compete is something coaches look for on day one.
